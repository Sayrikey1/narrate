# The Wealth Quadrants

[@ 00:00]

[fast-paced][urgent] Your job is the single most expensive thing you will ever own.

Not your car. Not your rent. Not that ridiculous coffee habit that every finance guy on this platform wants to lecture you about. Your job. The thing your parents were proud of. The thing you put on your LinkedIn. The thing you rearranged your entire life around.

And here's the part that's going to make some of you angry enough to type something aggressive in the comments before you even finish this sentence. Good. Do it. It helps the video. The plan you were handed was not designed to make you rich. It was designed to make you predictable.

Go to school. Get good grades. Get a safe job. Save your money. Diversify. Retire at sixty-five and finally, finally, do the things you actually wanted to do, assuming your body still works.

That's not a financial plan. That's a forty-year payment plan for a life you rent back from other people, one weekend at a time.

[intense] And I need you to hear the nuance before you rage-quit. Because I'm not the guy who's going to tell you education is worthless, or that you should burn your job down on a Tuesday afternoon. I'm going to tell you something worse. Something more uncomfortable.

You were taught how to make money. Nobody ever taught you how money is made.

Those are two completely different subjects. And confusing them is why doctors go broke, why lawyers have negative net worth at forty, and why the guy who fixes their air conditioning quietly retires first.

[building energy] For the next twenty-four minutes, I'm going to show you the four ways money is made on this planet. Why two of them will drain you until there's nothing left. And the exact sequence for crossing from one side to the other. Let's go.

[@ 01:10]

[fast-paced][urgent] In 1998, Robert Kiyosaki, the guy who wrote Rich Dad Poor Dad, the book that's been sitting on your shelf half-read since 2019, released the follow-up. And honestly? The follow-up is the better book. It's called Cashflow Quadrant, and it contains the single most useful diagram in personal finance.

Here it is. One cross. Four boxes. Every dollar earned by every human being on earth comes from one of these four positions.

Top left. E. Employee. You have a job. Bottom left. S. Self-employed. Or small business owner. Or specialist. You have a practice. Top right. B. Business owner. You own a system, and people work in it. Bottom right. I. Investor. Your money works, and you don't.

Now here's the thesis of this entire video. And if you take nothing else, take this.

The line down the middle of that diagram is the most important line in your financial life. It is more important than your salary. More important than your degree. More important than your budget, your side hustle, your interest rate, and every single thing your bank's mobile app is trying to gamify you into caring about.

[intense] Because the left side of that line and the right side of that line are not two levels of the same game. They're two different games. Different rules. Different tax code. Different definition of the word risk. Different definition of the word asset.

Two people can make the exact same two hundred thousand a year. One of them is free in eight years. The other one is trapped forever. The difference isn't discipline. It's which box the money came out of.

Let's start on the left. Where almost all of you are standing right now.

[SFX: office ambience, keyboards and low chatter, 6s, loop]

[@ 03:00]

[fast-paced][urgent] The Employee is defined by one word, and Kiyosaki nails it in the book. Security.

Watch how an E talks. Listen to the vocabulary. Guaranteed. Benefits. Stable. Pension. Permanent. An E doesn't primarily want money. An E wants the absence of uncertainty. And they will pay enormous sums of money for it, they just pay in a currency that doesn't show up on any statement.

Now let me be extremely clear, because the comment section is already warming up. There is nothing morally wrong with being an employee. Nurses are employees. Air traffic controllers are employees. The engineers who keep the electricity in your house are employees. Society does not function without the E quadrant, and anyone who sneers at it is selling you a course.

The problem isn't the job. The problem is the math of the position.

Here's what actually happens to an employee's money, in order. You work. Your employer pays the government. Then you get paid. Then you pay your rent, your car, your food, your insurance. And then, with whatever fumes are left at the bottom of the tank, you're told to invest for the future.

You are last in line to your own labor. Every single month. And you are the only participant in that chain who cannot legally rearrange the order.

Second problem. Your income has a hard ceiling that has nothing to do with your ability. There are only so many hours. You cannot work four hundred hours a month. You cannot clone yourself. Your best possible outcome is trading a premium hour for a premium rate. And the moment you stop trading, the money stops. Not slows. Stops.

[SFX: treadmill belt running at pace, 5s, loop]

[intense] Third problem. And this is the one that actually keeps me up at night. Your job is a single-customer business, where the customer can fire you, and you cannot fire them.

Think about that. If I told you I ran a company where one hundred percent of revenue came from a single client, who could cancel the contract without notice, at any time, for reasons entirely outside my control, you would say that's the most fragile business you've ever heard of. You would call it reckless. And you'd be right.

That's your job. That's the safe option. Ask anyone who lived through a corporate restructuring, an efficiency initiative, or a Tuesday morning calendar invite titled "quick sync."

Security, it turns out, was never a property of the job. It was a feeling the job gave you, while quietly removing every other option you had.

[SFX: phone notification chime, single, 2s]

[@ 06:30]

[fast-paced][urgent] So the smart ones figure this out. They read the book, they get furious, they quit, they go independent. Freelance. Consultant. Agency. Barbershop. Restaurant. Solo law practice. Six-figure creator. Welcome to the S quadrant.

Kiyosaki's line for the S is that they don't own a business, the business owns them. And I want to update that for today, because the modern S quadrant is more seductive than it's ever been.

The modern S has a Stripe dashboard and a personal brand. They post about freedom. They have clients, not a boss. And on paper, they're winning. They might be out-earning their old salary two to one.

But run the diagnostic. It's one question, and it's brutal. If you disappeared for ninety days, what happens to the money?

If the answer is "it stops," you did not buy a business. You bought yourself a job. One with no HR, no sick pay, no severance, unpredictable income, and a boss who is now emotionally incapable of letting you rest. Because that boss is you.

And here's the trap that makes the S quadrant so sticky. The S is good at the work. That's why they left. The best designer becomes a freelance designer. The best chef opens a restaurant. The best developer starts consulting. Their competence is the entire product.

Which means every dollar of growth requires more of the one thing that cannot scale. Them.

So they raise rates. Good move, for a while. Then they hit the ceiling where clients won't pay more, so they hire. But they hire helpers, not replacements, because nobody can do it like they can. Now they're doing the work and managing people who do the work badly. Now they're the bottleneck in their own company. Every decision routes through them. They can't take a holiday without the revenue dipping.

The S built a cage, and signed the lease themselves.

And the cruelest part? The S quadrant is often taxed the hardest of anyone on this diagram, while carrying all the risk of ownership, and none of the leverage.

That's the left side. E trades security for a ceiling. S trades a boss for a bottleneck. Both are selling time. The only negotiation is the price.

So let's talk about the people who stopped selling time entirely.

[SFX: server room hum, steady, 6s, loop]

[@ 10:00]

[fast-paced][urgent] The B quadrant is where the language changes completely.

Kiyosaki's distinction between S and B is the sharpest idea in the whole book, and it's this. The S owns a job. The B owns a system. An S is the most important person in their business. A B has deliberately engineered themselves into being the least necessary person in theirs.

Test it with the same question. If a real B disappears for ninety days, orders still get fulfilled. Support tickets still get answered. Payroll still runs. Revenue still lands. Because the B didn't build a service. They built a machine that produces the service, and then hired operators for it.

Working in it, versus working on it.

Now here's where I have to update the book. Because 1998 didn't have what we have. In Kiyosaki's era, reaching the B quadrant essentially meant franchising, manufacturing, or building a real estate operation. Enormous capital. Enormous overhead.

Today, leverage got democratized. And this is genuinely the most under-appreciated fact of our economic era.

Software that gets built once and sold ten thousand times while you're asleep. A productized service, where the process is documented so precisely that a trained contractor delivers it identically every time. A media asset, a channel, a newsletter, a catalogue, that keeps distributing after you've stopped producing. A community with recurring billing. An automation stack, where the thing that used to require four employees now requires one operator and a well-built workflow.

That's the actual definition of the B quadrant, stripped of the mystique. Income that is decoupled from your presence.

And it runs on two engines the left side can't touch. Kiyosaki calls them O.P.T. and O.P.M. Other People's Time. You buy an hour for fifty and it produces two hundred, and you can buy a thousand of those hours. You can't work a thousand hours. And Other People's Money. Investor capital, business credit, supplier terms, customer prepayments.

The left side is taught that all debt is evil. The right side learns to distinguish between debt that buys depreciating comfort, and debt that buys a productive asset. Those are not the same instrument.

And then there's the tax code. Which is not a punishment. It's a set of instructions.

Broadly, and specifics vary by country, so this is education, not advice, talk to an actual accountant in your jurisdiction. An employee earns, gets taxed, then spends. A business earns, spends on legitimate business expenses, and is taxed on what's left. Same activity. Different order of operations.

Governments write those incentives deliberately. Because businesses create jobs, housing, and infrastructure. The tax code pays people to do the things the government would otherwise have to do itself.

The left side reads the tax code as a bill. The right side reads it as a map.

[SFX: low sustained drone, warm, 6s, loop]

[@ 13:30]

[fast-paced][urgent] And then there's the final box. The one almost nobody actually reaches. And the one everybody thinks they're in.

The Investor. Where your money is the employee.

Let me kill a misconception immediately. Contributing to a workplace retirement fund does not make you an I. That makes you an E with a savings habit. Which is good, keep doing it, seriously, the employer match is free money and refusing it is financial self-harm. But it isn't this quadrant.

A saver hopes. An investor underwrites.

The I quadrant is defined by control and analysis. You evaluate an asset. You understand how it produces cash. You know what breaks it. You know how you get out. And you make a decision. That's the job. And the job is largely mental, which is why it's the only quadrant that gets easier as you get older and worse at everything else.

Modern version, again. Index funds and dividend equities at the passive end. Rental property and REITs. Private credit. Equity in other people's companies, which is why founders who exit become angel investors. They're not being charitable. They're changing quadrants. Royalties. Licensing. Even the boring stuff, treasuries, bonds, when the math says so.

And notice what's not there. Not lottery tickets. Not leveraged crypto positions taken at two in the morning because a stranger with an anime profile picture told you it was going to ten x. That's not the I quadrant. That's the E quadrant with a gambling problem.

Speculation is not investment.

[intense] Here's the reason B and I are stacked on the same side of the line. The B quadrant is the engine that funds the I quadrant. That's the sequence almost nobody explains. A business generates surplus cash. That surplus buys assets. Those assets generate income that doesn't require the business. And at that exact moment, when your assets cover your cost of living, you become genuinely, structurally free.

Passive income greater than expenses. That's the whole equation.

Not rich. Not successful. Free. And notice how low that bar might actually be for some of you watching. It's not ten million dollars. It's a number you could probably write on the back of your hand right now.

So how do you get across the line? That's the next six minutes. And it's the reason you clicked.

[fast-paced][direct] Okay. Rage-bait's over. Let's do the actual work. Because the internet is full of people who'll tell you the quadrants exist, and nobody who tells you the sequence. Six steps. In order. Don't skip.

Step one. Do not quit your job.

I know. Every guru tells you to burn the boats. Don't.

Your paycheck is the most patient, lowest-cost startup funding you will ever have access to. No investor will give you capital with no equity, no board seat, and no deadline. Your employer does that every two weeks.

So reframe the job. It is not your identity, and it is not your prison. It is a funding round, with a runway you control. And meanwhile, extract everything else from it that's non-monetary. The skills. The systems knowledge. The relationships. The understanding of how a real operation actually works. You are being paid to attend business school.

The people who cross successfully almost always build in the overlap. The ones who blow up are the ones who quit at zero revenue, with three months of savings and a vibe.

Step two. Build a runway before you build a business. Six months of living expenses in cash. Boring. Non-negotiable.

Not because I'm the emergency fund police. But because of what desperation does to judgment. A broke founder takes bad clients, signs bad terms, prices in fear, and accepts the first offer on the table. Money in the bank isn't safety. It's optionality. It's the ability to say no. Which is the single highest-return skill in business.

Runway doesn't reduce your risk. It increases the quality of every decision you'll make for the next two years.

Step three. Enter through S. But design for B from day one.

Here's the honest bit nobody says. Almost nobody teleports from E to B. You will probably pass through S. You'll trade your time for the first money, because your skill is the only asset you own right now. That's fine. That's the on-ramp.

The trap is staying there. So from the first day, you build differently.

Document everything. Every time you do a task twice, write down how you did it. That document is the raw material of a system. And systems are the only thing that separates S from B.

Then, sell an outcome. Not your hours. The instant you bill by the hour, you've capped yourself, and you've made yourself the product. Package it. Fixed scope. Fixed price. Defined result.

And be specific to the point of discomfort. "Freelance marketer" is an S forever. "We run paid acquisition for dental clinics, one system, same process every time," that's a machine waiting to be built. Because the repetition is what makes delegation possible.

[SFX: mechanical ratchet clicking, 3s]

[@ 20:15]

[fast-paced][direct] Step four. Buy back your time, in the correct order. This is where the S quadrant kills people. And it's a mechanical fix.

List everything you did last week. Every task. Put a rough hourly value next to each one. Admin, scheduling, invoicing, inbox, formatting, that's ten dollar work. Sales, product, strategy, key relationships, that's thousand dollar work.

Now the rule. You delegate from the bottom up. Permanently. Forever. Buy back the cheapest hour first, with the surplus the business generates. Then the next cheapest. Every hour you reclaim gets redeployed into the thousand dollar column. Which produces more surplus. Which buys back the next hour.

That's the actual mechanism of crossing the line. It isn't a leap. It's a ratchet.

And most people never start it, because the first hire feels like a cost. It isn't a cost. It's a purchase. You're buying inventory of the only non-renewable resource you have.

Step five. Redefine risk. Or you'll never move.

The left side thinks risk is a property of the thing. Business is risky. Jobs are safe. Stocks are risky. Savings are safe.

The right side knows risk is a property of the operator. Handing your life savings to a stranger's guaranteed thirty percent monthly scheme is insane. Buying a business you've studied for eighteen months, whose numbers you've audited, in an industry you've worked in, that's a calculated position. Same category. Opposite risk profile. And the variable is what you know.

So manage it like an adult. Never bet money you can't survive losing. Assume the first venture fails, most do, and size it so failure is tuition, not catastrophe. Get out of high-interest consumer debt before you chase leverage, because that debt eats returns faster than any asset can generate them. And verify with your own eyes. If someone's pitch requires urgency and discourages questions, they're not offering you an asset. They're offering you a loss, with extra steps.

And nothing on this channel is personalised financial advice. Get an accountant in your jurisdiction. The tax code is a map, but you need a local guide.

Step six. Convert cash into assets. Relentlessly.

The final step is the one that separates the temporarily rich from the permanently wealthy. And it's the one the flashy ones skip.

The B quadrant produces income. Income is fragile. Markets rotate. Platforms change their algorithms. Industries get disrupted. A global event closes your sector overnight. If the business is all you have, you're one bad year from the left side.

So the surplus doesn't become a car. It becomes an asset. Every month. Mechanically. Boringly. Without debate.

The business feeds the investor. And the investor never gets fired.

Do that long enough, and one morning you'll notice your assets cover your life. You didn't get lucky. You just ran the ratchet.
