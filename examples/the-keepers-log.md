# The Keeper's Log

[SFX: distant foghorn, single low blast, 4s]

The light at Ardnamurchan turned for the last time on a Tuesday in March, and
nobody came to watch. There was no ceremony. A man from the board drove up the
single-track road, climbed the ninety-two steps, and switched it off with the
same small brass lever that had switched it on every evening for a hundred and
forty years.

[SFX: heavy iron door closing, echo in a stone tower, 3s]

[@ 00:26]

What replaced it was better. That is the part nobody argues with. The automated
lamp is brighter, it never sleeps, and it costs almost nothing to run. It does
not need a keeper, or a keeper's cottage, or the boat that brought the keeper's
groceries out every second Thursday.

[SFX: wind over open water, 30s, loop]

But something did go, and it is harder to name than the light. The keeper kept
a log. Not a poetic thing — a ruled book, one line a day, weather and wick and
hours burned. Wind north-north-east. Visibility two miles. Lamp lit at four
minutes past five.

[@ 01:02]

Read a hundred years of those lines and you get something no instrument
records: a place, described by someone who could not leave it. Gannets early
this year. Swell from the west, no wind behind it, which is the sort that
catches boats out. Mrs. Anderson's roof went in the night.

None of that is weather data. All of it is information.

[SFX: pages turning slowly, close, 3s]

The last keeper was a woman called Morag Cameron, and she wrote her final
entry the night before the man with the brass lever arrived. It is four words
long. Nothing to report tonight.

She had been there nineteen years. The log runs to thirty-eight volumes, and
they are all in a climate-controlled room in Edinburgh now, catalogued and
digitised and read by roughly four people a year.

[@ 01:36]

[SFX: waves breaking on rock, mid-distance, 6s]

The tower still stands. You can visit it, and a recorded voice will tell you
about the engineering, which is genuinely remarkable. What it will not tell you
is that for a century and a half, on this exact rock, somebody stayed awake so
that somebody else could get home.

That is not in the log either. It is the reason for it.
