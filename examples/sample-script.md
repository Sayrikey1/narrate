# The Longest Winter

Nobody in the village could remember a winter like it. The snow began in
October, three weeks early, and by the second week of November the road to the
valley had disappeared entirely — not blocked, not impassable, simply gone,
folded under a whiteness that made the whole landscape look unfinished.

Dr. Halvorsen kept a record. She had kept one every year since 1987, in the
same water-stained notebook, and her entries that season grew steadily shorter.
"Snow," she wrote on the fourteenth. "More snow," on the fifteenth. By the end
of the month she had stopped writing words at all and simply drew a small
horizontal line, one per day, the way a prisoner might.

What she did not write down, because she did not yet know it, was that the
temperature had not risen above freezing for forty-one consecutive days. That
number would matter later. It would appear in three separate scientific
papers, in a documentary, and eventually on a small brass plaque bolted to the
wall of the village hall — but in the moment it was just weather, and weather
is only history in retrospect.

The first sign that something had genuinely changed came from the birds.

Erik Lindqvist noticed it on a Tuesday. He was seventy-one years old and had
fed the same population of jackdaws from the same windowsill for the better
part of four decades, and on that Tuesday morning there were none. Not fewer.
None. He waited an hour, then two, then put the bread away and sat down and
did not get up again for a long time.

It is worth pausing here to say something about jackdaws. They are corvids —
cousins to crows and ravens — and they are extraordinarily reluctant to
abandon a territory. A jackdaw colony will hold its ground through drought,
through construction, through the arrival of predators. They do not leave.
When they leave, something is deeply wrong.

By December the researchers had arrived. Three of them, in a vehicle
manifestly unsuited to the conditions, carrying equipment that measured things
the villagers had never thought to measure. They set up instruments on the
ridge. They took core samples. They asked a great many questions and answered
almost none.

The youngest of them, a graduate student named Aditi Rao, would later write
the paper that made the village briefly famous. But that was two years away,
and in December she was mostly cold, and mostly ignored, and spent her
evenings in the one room the guesthouse had left, reading the same paperback
over and over because she had brought only the one.

What the instruments found was this: the ground was warm.

Not warm to the touch — nothing so dramatic. But eleven metres down, in the
permafrost that had held the valley's shape since the last glaciation, the
temperature had risen by one and a half degrees. It had been rising, the core
samples suggested, for approximately thirty years. Slowly, invisibly, and
entirely without anyone noticing.

The snow, it turned out, was a symptom rather than a cause. Warmer ground
meant more moisture in the air, and more moisture in the air over a landscape
still cold enough to freeze it meant snow — an enormous amount of snow, far
more than the region had any historical business receiving.

The jackdaws had not fled the cold. They had fled the wet.

Dr. Halvorsen lived long enough to read the paper. She is quoted in it, once,
in a footnote, and the quotation is characteristically brief. Asked whether
she had suspected anything, she said: "I write down what happens. I don't
write down what it means. That's somebody else's job."

She was ninety-two when she died, and her notebooks — thirty-eight of them,
water-stained, precise, entirely unsentimental — are now held in the regional
archive. Researchers still use them. The 1987 volume is the most requested
item in the collection.

The road to the valley was cleared in April, later than anyone could recall.
The jackdaws came back the following spring, though not in the same numbers,
and not to the same windowsill. Erik Lindqvist had died in February. His
daughter kept feeding them for a while, out of a sense of obligation she
couldn't quite articulate, and then one day she stopped, and nobody noticed
that either.

Winters since have been ordinary. That is, they have been warmer — steadily,
unremarkably warmer, in the way that does not make anybody keep a notebook.
