Look out of an aeroplane window and you are looking through a shape that was
paid for in lives. The rounded corner is not a style choice. It is a correction.

In 1952 the de Havilland Comet became the first jet airliner in commercial
service, and by every measure that mattered then it was a triumph. It flew at
500 miles per hour, roughly twice the speed of the propeller aircraft it
replaced, and it cruised at 35,000 feet, above most of the weather. Passengers
described the ride as uncannily smooth.

Then, in the space of twelve months, three of them came apart in the air.

The first was in May 1953, near Calcutta. The second, in January 1954, off
Elba. The third, in April, near Naples. Fifty-six people died in the last two
alone. The fleet was grounded, and the investigation that followed is still
taught, because of how it was done.

Investigators recovered roughly two thirds of the Elba aircraft from the
seabed, extraordinary for the era. In parallel, engineers submerged an intact
Comet fuselage in a water tank and pressurised it over and over, simulating
flight after flight. After the equivalent of approximately 3,000 flights, the
tank fuselage failed.

It failed at a corner. Specifically, at the corner of a window.

The Comet's windows were square, or near enough. A square corner concentrates
stress: the load has nowhere to spread, so it piles up in a very small area. On
the ground that is fine. But a pressurised cabin inflates slightly at altitude
and relaxes on descent, every single flight, and metal that is flexed enough
times will crack no matter how strong it is. Engineers call it metal fatigue,
and in 1952 it was understood in theory far better than it was in practice.

A curve has no such corner. Spread the same load around an arc and the stress
distributes instead of accumulating. That is the whole idea.

De Havilland never recovered its lead; Boeing took it with the 707. But every
pressurised aircraft built since, by anyone, has rounded windows, and the
reason is a water tank in Farnborough and three aircraft that should have come
home.

Next time you are at 35,000 feet, put a finger on that curve. It is one of the
few pieces of engineering you can actually touch that exists because somebody
worked out, at real cost, exactly why the obvious shape was wrong.
